# Safety & Privacy Quickstart for Cleanup Organizers

This is a **practical, copy-pasteable safety and privacy guide** for organizers. You can read it in 5–10 minutes and adapt it to your own event.

It is meant to work alongside your local laws, common sense, and any official guidance from your city or parks department. When in doubt, **follow local rules and prioritize human safety over finishing the cleanup.**

---

## 1. Core Principles (Print or Read Aloud)

You can use this block directly in your event description or day-of briefing:

> **Our cleanup is people-first.**
> - We clean **trash and hazards**, not people.
> - We do **not** target or displace unhoused neighbors or anyone spending time in the park.
> - We do **not** handle sharps (needles), medical waste, or suspicious chemicals ourselves – we **mark and report** them.
> - We respect **privacy and consent** in photos and stories.
> - It is always okay to **step back, ask for help, or leave** if you feel unsafe.

---

## 2. Quick Safety Checklist (Organizers)

Use this the day before and morning of the event.

**Before the event:**

- [ ] Check the **weather** and adjust timing, clothing guidance, or cancel if conditions are unsafe.
- [ ] Confirm any **permits or approvals** needed from your city or parks department.
- [ ] Make sure you have basic **supplies**:
  - [ ] Trash bags (sturdy, 30-gallon if possible)
  - [ ] Gloves (work or disposable)
  - [ ] A few **trash grabbers/litter pickers** if possible
  - [ ] Hand sanitizer or access to soap and water
  - [ ] A small **first aid kit** (bandages, antiseptic wipes, etc.)
- [ ] Decide on a clear **meeting point** and how you will describe it to volunteers.
- [ ] Draft a **2–3 sentence safety note** for your reminder email or message (see examples below).

**At the event (before you start):**

- [ ] Do a **quick scan** of the area for obvious hazards (needles, broken glass, large metal pieces, etc.).
- [ ] Choose a **safety lead** (this can be you) who:
  - introduces the safety rules,
  - keeps an eye on conditions,
  - is the point person if something feels unsafe.
- [ ] Make sure volunteers know:
  - where to put full bags,
  - who to talk to if they feel unwell or unsafe,
  - that it’s okay to take breaks or leave at any time.

## 3. What *Not* to Pick Up (Hazards)

Volunteers should **stop and call it in** (or tell the organizer) instead of directly handling:

- Needles or syringes
- Other sharps (razor blades, broken glass in piles, knives, etc.)
- Medical or biological waste (bloody items, bandages, masks soaked in fluids)
- Suspicious chemicals (unlabeled bottles, strong chemical smell, leaking containers)
- Heavy, sharp, or unstable objects (large metal pieces, heavy appliances, mattresses)

**Safer pattern:**

1. **Mark the spot** from a distance (cone, stick, or a mental note + nearby landmark).
2. **Inform the organizer/safety lead.**
3. Use a **non-emergency reporting channel** (311, local parks hotline, etc.) if available.
4. Reserve **911** for serious medical emergencies, imminent danger, or likely overdoses.

If at any point the area feels too risky, it’s okay to **scale down** or **end the cleanup early**.

---

## 4. Non-Carceral, People-First Norms

These norms protect your neighbors and help avoid causing harm while trying to help.

- **We clean trash, not people.** Your mission is to remove litter and hazards, not to police or remove people.
- Do **not** throw away:
  - tents, carts, bikes,
  - bedding, clothing piles,
  - backpacks or bags that look like personal belongings.
- Do **not** call police just because someone is unhoused, loitering, or visibly using the space.
- If there is a conflict or someone is upset:
  - stay calm, listen briefly,
  - step back and give space,
  - end or move the cleanup if needed.

You can say to volunteers:

> "Our focus today is trash and safety hazards. We are **not** here to judge or move anyone. If there’s a conflict or something feels off, find me and we’ll step back and reassess."

## 5. Privacy & Data: Handling Volunteer Info

Most cleanups will use some kind of signup form. Treat that data as private.

**Do this:**

- Use a **private spreadsheet or form** system you control (Google Forms, etc.).
- Collect only what you need (e.g., name, email; emergency contact if you choose).
- Keep the raw signups **out of public repos and websites.**

**Do *not* do this:**

- Don’t commit or upload:
  - raw signup spreadsheets,
  - RSVP lists,
  - survey responses with names or contact details.
- Don’t publish documents that include:
  - personal emails or phone numbers,
  - home addresses,
  - private social handles.

When you share results publicly (social media, reports, GitHub, etc.), use **approximate, aggregate stats** instead of row-by-row data:

- "about 5 volunteers" instead of a named list.
- "~180 gallons of trash (~6 large bags)" instead of exact counts tied to specific people.
- "about an hour of cleanup" instead of logging individual arrival/departure times.

## 6. Photos, Video, and Evidence

Good documentation helps show impact without exposing people.

**Safer defaults for photos and video:**

- Focus on **trash piles, bags, and park areas** (before/after shots).
- Aim **away from faces**, license plates, and private homes.
- Avoid photographing **encampments or clearly lived-in spaces**.
- If someone is clearly recognizable, **ask for consent** before publishing their image.

**What not to publish in public repos or websites:**

- Close-up faces of volunteers or bystanders (unless you have explicit consent and are sure it’s safe).
- License plates, house numbers, or easily identifiable home entrances.
- Photos that show where specific unhoused people sleep or store belongings.

When in doubt, it’s better to crop, blur, or choose a different photo.

## 7. Sample Safety Language You Can Reuse

You can adapt these snippets into your own messages.

### Signup Form or Event Description

> **Safety & Respect:** We’ll provide trash bags and basic supplies. Please wear closed-toe shoes and weather-appropriate clothing. We do **not** handle needles, medical waste, or suspicious chemicals — we mark and report hazards instead. Our focus is cleaning trash and making the park safer, **not** policing or displacing anyone.

### Reminder Email (Day Before)

> Quick reminder for tomorrow’s cleanup:
> - Wear comfortable, closed-toe shoes and clothes you don’t mind getting dirty.
> - Bring water and sunscreen if you can.
> - If you see needles, medical waste, or anything that feels unsafe, **do not touch it** — flag it for an organizer.
> - It’s always okay to take breaks or leave early if you need to.

### Day-of Verbal Briefing (1–2 minutes)

> "Thanks for coming! Quick safety briefing:
> - We’re here to clean **trash and hazards**, not people.
> - If you see needles, medical waste, or anything that feels sketchy, **don’t touch it**. Get me or another organizer; we’ll mark and report it.
> - Please be respectful of everyone using the park, including unhoused neighbors.
> - It’s completely fine to take breaks, switch tasks, or stop early. If at any point you feel unsafe, tell us and we’ll adjust or wrap up."

Feel free to print this page or save a copy in your own notes app to read from.

## 8. If Something Goes Wrong

Even with planning, things can go sideways. A simple response plan helps:

- **Medical issue or serious injury?**
  - Call your local emergency number (e.g., 911) if needed.
  - Stay calm, keep the area clear, and follow dispatcher instructions.

- **Conflict or harassment?**
  - Avoid escalating. Step back, apologize if needed, and move the group to another area or end the event.

- **Hazard you can’t resolve?**
  - Mark the area if it’s safe to do so.
  - Use non-emergency channels (311, park hotline, city service request app) to report it.

Afterwards, make a brief private note for yourself so you can improve your plan for next time.
